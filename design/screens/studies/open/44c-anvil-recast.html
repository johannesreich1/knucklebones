What it says: this die is rolled again. The spell's own word is recast, and the game already has an animation for a die being cast: the stage die's tumble, and the board's settle bounce when one lands. This is that vocabulary applied where it belongs instead of a new one invented beside it.
It teaches by rhyme. A player who has watched the die in play roll knows what a tumbling die means by their second turn — the shape carries the meaning before the rule is read, which is the cheapest teaching there is.
The mark is the tumble about to happen — the chosen die rocking on its corner, plus the rune's ring turning around it. It says "this one is going back in" without adding a single new object.
Cost, and it is a real lie: a roll is random, and ANVIL is not. The new face is the die in hand, known before the tap, and an animation that spins and lands is telling the player the result was drawn. That is the opposite of the promise the rune is built on ("one tap, and the player can always predict the answer"). It may be rescuable by ending the tumble on the hand die's face with the hand die visibly emptying — but as drawn, this is the idea most likely to be measured and thrown away.
