What it says: a full column is held — a hard rim with the four corners clamped shut, and behind the dice a comb of hexagons filling the gaps the dice leave. That is the whole rule of COLUMN SHIELD in one picture: the column is full, so it is locked, and it stays locked as long as it stays full. Nothing is spent, nothing depletes, and it cannot break. What changed since the haze: the layer in front of the dice is gone, and nothing took its place. The last draft had exactly one — a 3%-to-6% gradient over the whole stack — and that layer was this card's entire pip-contrast bill. It is deleted, not thinned; the motes went with it, because they were the mist's weather and there is no mist now. What is left in front of a die is what was there on a bare column: nothing. The rim, the corners and the ring a strike leaves are lines at the stack's edge and cross no die at any frame. This draft puts less ink in front of the dice than the one before it, which was the condition on adding the hexagons at all. Where the structure went instead: behind them, at z 0, into the only room a lattice can take without covering anything — the two 6px seams between the dice and the 9px margin inside the rim. Four courses of ten hexagons, one size, one orientation, drawn the way the ward shell (W5) draws its scales — a 0.7px stroke, one custom property per polygon for its delay. Stroke-only at rest, which is what makes it affordable: a honeycomb of sub-pixel lines covers a fraction of what a wash does, so it can be structure without being a layer. When a hexagon is LIT it fills, to 34% for a fraction of a second, and then empties back to its stroke — that flash is the beat, and it is free here for the same reason everything else is: every polygon sits in a strip no die covers, so a fill costs no pip contrast either. Measured: a hexagon is 5.2 across and 4.5 tall, so with its stroke it takes 5.2 of a seam's 6px and clears the die above and the die below by 0.4px each; in the two margins the clearance is 1.4px; and each course's end vertices land on x 9 and x 61, the dice's own width. The wash came down to make room: 13% flat is now 5%, and the ring pulled in from the boundary 18% → 9%. Not for contrast — the wash is behind the dice and never cost any — but because a 13% field is brighter than a 0.7px stroke, and the comb would have read as texture inside a haze instead of as the thing you are looking at. 5% is the amount that still says the rim encloses something; the strokes say what. If it reads at all beside them, take it to zero. At rest it is quiet on purpose: 62% of the hue on a 0.7px line held at 26% opacity — you sense a structure behind the column without being able to read it. That number is free: the comb never touches a die, so it could go to 100% without costing a pip. It is held down because a guard that can never break should be the calm thing until something tests it. The beats. A column fills away from its mouth — render.ts maps board index 0 to the slot nearest the chip, so the die that closes a column is the far one. The corners clamp, the rim lights, and the comb knits from that far end back toward the mouth, a course every 0.05s: the structure appears after the column is full, never before it, and the last hexagon settles at 39.4% of the loop, inside the 40% after which nothing moves at all. Then a strike arrives at the mouth and fails. It lights the course nearest the contact first and runs down and outward, 0.025s a ring — and every hexagon fills and then empties back to the 26% it started at. Nothing drains, nothing cracks, no count goes down. The last frame of the strike loop is byte-for-byte the first, and the column beside it — the same column afterwards, drawn static — is identical on purpose. The chip is untouched. It keeps its box, its score and its mark — measured constant at every frame of every beat. Two layers do travel under it: the strike's ring, and the corner clamps as they spring in. They pass BENEATH the chip (z 4 and 5 against the chip's 6) and never over it, so the chip is never covered or dimmed — but "nothing reaches it" would be the wrong claim, and the distinction is the one that matters if either layer is ever given a higher z. The rim stops 5px below the chip's underside and the topmost hexagon 7.4px below it, and the mark stays absolutely positioned at the chip's right end where main.css puts it (.chip .sh, right:4px), so a score that changes width re-centres under a mark that never moves. Costs. Forty stroked polygons per shielded column, and it is per column: three of them is 120, on a board that already carries a bloom per die — worth a frame check on the oldest phone before this ships, though strokes that only change opacity and fill are the cheap kind. The seam sets the hexagon's size, and it is small: 5.2px across is what fits a 6px gap once the stroke is counted, roughly seven tenths the width of the ward shell's scales. If the gutters do grow, the comb grows with them, and that is the first thing to re-measure. And the rim still wants room the board has not got: it stands 9px off the dice while columns sit 6px apart and the chip strip clears the board by 4px, so two shielded neighbours would overlap by 12px. Either both gutters grow in this mode, or the rim tucks in to 3px off the dice and gives up the inner shelf that makes its edge look thick. This draft does not settle that trade — it only stops the guard from costing anything the dice were paying.
