What it says: somebody is being robbed. Of the six this is the only one whose frames admit that the die belonged to a person sitting opposite you: it resists, the column strains after it, and it tears free. Everything else here moves a token.
That is either the best or the worst thing about it, and the design doc is unusually direct about which side of the line PILFER stands on: destroyed dice are already in Knucklebones' emotional vocabulary; stolen dice are not — which is why the rune is capped at ONE die and why it is the roster's only aggressive option. An animation that leans into the taking makes that cap feel earned. It can equally make a local two-player game unpleasant, and that is not decidable from a card.
The arrival has a jolt — the only one in this set. It is a knowing risk: a die that thumps down looks like a die that struck, and PILFER's landing must strike nothing. If it is kept it needs to be a squash on the die alone, never a board shake and never a burst, or it will teach the wrong rule.
Cost: the most frames of the six and the least reusable — none of it survives being applied to a future rune. It also fights the take-back rule's spirit: a cast this theatrical will make a player want it back, and a board cast is final by construction.
