The collision, and how it is solved: the shell no longer arcs ACROSS the column's mouth — it is seated inside it. Its two feet stand on the column's own walls half way down the top cell, and its crown reaches 1.5px above the top die — with the topmost scale stopping 2.8px short of the chip's underside, so 2.8px of the chip's 7px of clear air is left standing at the worst frame of the worst beat: the chip is the lintel the arch presses into, never a thing it covers, dims or moves. Every transform in the beats is anchored at the feet, so even the brace and the burn travel downward, away from the score. The old draft needed ~30px of reserved lane over every mouth — the same lane the chip strip already occupies — and that bill is now zero. The chip is drawn faithfully here so the claim can be checked: one column wide, points in the side's colour, guard marks absolutely positioned at the ends (main.css puts the shield at right:4px and the ward at left:4px for exactly this reason). Third row: two chips, 5 and 17×2, identical shells — the score re-centres, the shell cannot move, because it is anchored to the column and knows nothing about the chip. That second chip is also the tightest the box ever gets: a two-digit score with a ×k badge comes within about 2px of the 13px mark at the end. That squeeze is the chip's own, it is there today, and the shell neither causes it nor helps it. Over the dice: a 52px die is blank glass for its first 8px. The arc — a half-ellipse 26×27.5 about the column's centre — clears the nearest pip by 5.1px, and the nine scales, riding on the shell's outer face, clear it by 3.0px. There is no body wash at all — a rim, a dim under-rim and a comb of scales at 12%. Pip contrast is what it is on a bare column. Why the ward and not the shield: a shell that flexes, hardens and burns out is the grammar of a thing that can be spent. COLUMN SHIELD is not spent — it is the state of being full, it engages the instant the column fills, and it cannot break — so it keeps exactly what it wears today, the gold mark at the chip's right end and the gold ring, and the two never read as one mechanism. The beats: your own die falls straight through the crown; the shell gives 2%, the scales light outward from the crossing, and nothing is spent, because a ward eats a strike and never refuses your placement. Then a strike arrives — drawn as a lance, not an enemy die, because the app never flies a die at your board and a 52px one would have to cross the chip to reach the mouth. It comes in at 42° onto the shell's right shoulder, so its whole path stays in the gutter beside the chip — the opposite end from the mark it is about to put out; the rim goes white, the strike is thrown back the way it came, and then the shell unravels from the point of contact, the ring leaves the column and the mark leaves the chip. Costs: the shell marks the mouth, so it says which column is guarded and not which dice — the shipped mint ring is kept for that, and both go out together on the discharge. The resting sheen is one stroke animating forever on every warded column. And landscape transposes the board: the chip moves to the column's inner end and the shell turns a quarter with it — the same relationship, one rotation, which is the part the old lane-above-the-mouth version could not have done at all.
