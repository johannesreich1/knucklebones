What it says: the two protections are different kinds of mark, not two colours of one — which is the correction this card owes. A shield is the state of being full: a closed double line, no seam, no segments, no charge. It is drawn the moment the third die seats, and a strike can only make it flare, because there is nothing on it to take away. A ward is one charge: the same line, single and thinner, held shut by one clasp — the only part of either seal that stirs at rest, so the eye is told in advance where it will give. The strike snaps the clasp, the line swings open and unwinds off the column, the rune burns off the chip, and the last second of the loop holds the true after-state: dice, no protection. The line sits outside the slots, so it costs the dice nothing — no tint on the faces, no pip contrast lost — and the difference between the two rules is structural, closed against clasped, rather than a matter of degree. Neither seal ever stops your own die; a ward eats a strike, not a placement. Cost: the thinnest of the six, and the first to lose to a small phone or a bright room. The clasp is nine pixels wide and carries the whole "this one can break" reading — if it fails, the card fails. Under reduced motion the bead stops and both beats go static: closed-against-clasped still says which is which, but the breaking becomes a before-and-after the player may never see happen.
